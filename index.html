Independent Research

Research

Two independent papers on the NBA's second apron — a set of financial and roster-building restrictions introduced in the league's 2023 Collective Bargaining Agreement. One paper examines how the second apron has influenced payroll allocation, roster construction, and team performance. The other examines how it affected playing time, roster roles, and roster composition at the player level.

The Opportunity Cost of Concentrating Payroll: How the NBA's Second Apron Reshapes Roster Construction

Uses a team-level panel (450 team-seasons, 2011–12 through 2025–26) and a player-level panel (3,645 player-seasons over the same period) to examine how the NBA's second apron has influenced payroll allocation, roster construction, and team performance.

Key findings:

  • Following the introduction of the second apron, a significantly larger share of teams adopted highly concentrated payroll structures, although league-wide average payroll concentration did not increase significantly.
  • Teams with more concentrated payrolls carry fewer mid-level veterans—not simply fewer minutes for the veterans they retain. A 10-percentage-point increase in payroll concentration predicts approximately 0.44 fewer mid-level veterans on the roster (p < 0.001).
  • Teams allocating a larger share of minutes to mid-level veterans win more games, even after controlling for total payroll and the quality of their highest-paid players. A 10-percentage-point increase in mid-level veteran minute share is associated with approximately a 2.8-percentage-point increase in winning percentage (p < 0.001).
  • Payroll concentration itself has no independent relationship with winning once player quality is taken into account — but that average masks a sharper pattern: an additional unit of star quality is worth less to teams that have already concentrated more of their payroll around a small core, and concentrating further becomes actively costly once that core is elite. Part of this cost runs through the same mid-level-veteran crowd-out documented above.
  • Concludes with a case study of the Boston Celtics' supermax commitment to Jaylen Brown — and the team's eventual 2026 trade of Brown for Paul George — examined through the lens of this tradeoff.

How Does the NBA's Second Apron Reshape Mid-Level Veteran Labor?

Uses the same player-level panel (3,645 player-seasons, 2011–12 through 2025–26) to estimate player fixed-effects models across three labor groups—high-salary veterans, mid-level veterans, and rookie-scale players—to examine how the second apron affected playing time, roster roles, and roster composition.

Key findings:

  • Following the introduction of the second apron, mid-level veterans played 3.2 fewer minutes per game (p = 0.001), started significantly less often (p = 0.007), and appeared in fewer games (p = 0.035), suggesting teams reduced their reliance on mid-level veterans league-wide.
  • High-salary veterans experienced only a modest decline in playing time (−0.74 MPG, p = 0.024), while rookie-scale players became more likely to start (p = 0.045), consistent with teams shifting opportunities toward cheaper labor.
  • The composition of the mid-level salary band shifted significantly toward younger, earlier-career players (χ² p = 0.005), driven primarily by more fourth-year rookies entering the band (37.5% vs. 29.3% pre-apron) rather than established veterans exiting.
  • Among players who remained in the mid-level salary band, average performance improved substantially, particularly among prime-age players (27–30), whose average BPM increased from −0.56 to +0.43. This is consistent with a selection effect in which the second apron raised the performance threshold required to command a mid-level contract.
